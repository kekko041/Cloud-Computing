# English Conversation Prep — Oral Exam (MIT-EPI)

The bando requires a conversation assessing English proficiency (expected level: at least B2) — this is a spoken interaction, not a written test. Below: likely question types, a vocabulary checklist, and a few model answers to internalize the *structure*, not to memorize word-for-word.

## How to approach it
- Speak in full sentences, not fragments. Short, grammatically correct sentences beat long, hesitant ones.
- If you don't know a word, **paraphrase** rather than switching to Italian or freezing ("a document that proves your identity" instead of groping for "ID card").
- It's fine to ask for clarification: *"Could you repeat the question, please?"* or *"Do you mean X or Y?"* — this is itself evidence of working comprehension.
- Prepare a 60-90 second answer for "tell me about yourself / your background" — it's almost always the opener.

## Likely question categories

### 1. Personal / professional background
- *"Can you tell me about yourself and your professional background?"*
- *"Why did you apply for this position at the Ministry of Infrastructure and Transport?"*
- *"What are your main technical skills?"*

**Structure to use**: current role/background → relevant technical experience → why this role (link to public-sector IT, infrastructure digitalization) → one concrete example of work you're proud of.

### 2. Technical description (likely to overlap with the written/oral technical content)
- *"Can you explain what cloud computing is, in simple terms?"*
- *"What is the difference between a relational and a NoSQL database?"*
- *"How would you explain cybersecurity to a non-technical colleague?"*
- *"What is GDPR, and why does it matter for a public administration?"*

**Structure to use**: one-sentence definition → a simple real-world analogy → one concrete example.

**Useful vocabulary**: *scalability, reliability, encryption, data breach, compliance, stakeholder, legacy system, deployment, infrastructure, vulnerability, authentication vs authorization.*

### 3. Situational / opinion questions
- *"How do you keep your technical skills up to date?"*
- *"Describe a challenging technical problem you solved."*
- *"How do you handle working under a deadline?"*
- *"What do you think is the biggest challenge in digitalizing public administration?"*

**Structure to use (STAR-like, short)**: Situation → Action you took → Result/lesson learned. Keep it to 3-4 sentences unless asked to expand.

### 4. Current-affairs / sector-related small talk
- *"What do you know about recent developments in artificial intelligence regulation in Europe?"* (→ AI Act, risk-based approach)
- *"What do you think about cybersecurity threats to critical infrastructure?"* (→ NIS2, ransomware, critical infrastructure protection)

Keep answers grounded in what you already know technically (AI Act, NIS2, GDPR) — this doubles as a chance to show subject-matter competence *in English*, which is exactly what the examiners are checking.

## Sample 90-second answer (structure to adapt, not to memorize verbatim)
*"I have a background in IT, with hands-on experience in [your actual area — software development / systems administration / etc.]. Over the past few years I've worked on [one concrete project], where I was responsible for [role], and I had to deal with [one challenge, e.g. scalability, security]. I applied for this position because I'm interested in applying my technical skills to public-sector digitalization — I think infrastructure and transport is a field where good IT systems have a very direct impact on citizens' daily lives, from traffic monitoring to structural safety of bridges and roads. I'm particularly interested in [cybersecurity / data engineering / cloud architecture — pick what's true for you], and I believe this role would let me contribute while also learning a lot about how a large public administration manages complex technical systems."*
